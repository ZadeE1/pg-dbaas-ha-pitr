Revision 4 closed the data path, failover behaviour and backup design. Revision 5 removed the contradictions that had accumulated and answered every question that was still open. Revision 6 is a production-hardening pass for live data: the shared store now survives losing two machines instead of one, the gateway gained a connection pooler, the read path is wired rather than advertised, and the things a database layer cannot fix for itself — connection storms, certificate renewal, checkout idempotency — are decided instead of assumed. This project covers the database side only; what belongs to your application is drawn as a boundary in §04 and §15, not designed. What remains is implementation, and §14 says what that is.

Locked

Cloudflare Mesh, Layer 3. Docker on host networking. A reserved port range with no proxy. Strict synchronous replication, so an acknowledged order exists on two machines. One gateway sidecar per app. WAL-G to R2 with verification after every full.

Deliberate costs

Host networking needs a rootful daemon. Strict sync stops writes when one Pod is left. Surviving two host losses costs a fifth machine whose only job is holding a vote. Failover costs about forty seconds and every open connection.

Decided, not built

Control-plane authorisation. Secret delivery and rotation. Rolling major-version upgrades. Cluster deletion. Every one has a decision in §15; none has an implementation. That gap is the remaining work.

What changed in revisions 5 and 6, and why
  • Rev 6: five DCS voters instead of three, quorum three. Losing one host was always survivable; losing two stopped every cluster. The arithmetic that makes four voters a no-op is written into §15 so nobody has to re-derive it — five tolerates two, three losses is a restore.
  • Rev 6: PgBouncer now fronts HAProxy in the gateway container. PostgreSQL's default of 100 connections does not survive the two events this design cares about — a traffic spike, and a failover reconnect storm — and both happen while the database is healthy. One app port; HAProxy moved to loopback.
  • Rev 6: the read path is wired, with its limits stated. /ecom_ro on the same port routes catalogue reads to replicas, and the doc says plainly that it does not give read-your-writes.
  • Rev 6: certificate renewal is decided, and the line between this system and your application is drawn explicitly. Connection retries and checkout idempotency are recorded as requirements on the application, because strict sync is durable but not atomic and no database setting can stop a retried charge — but they are stated as a boundary, not designed here.
  • Header and status cards no longer contradict §13. Revision 4 said backups were undesigned in three places and then spent a hundred pages designing them.
  • §12: the gateway could not have found the primary. Every server line health-checked port 21001, which is only Pod 0's REST port. Two of three servers sit permanently down, and one of the two is the writer.
  • §00 / §04: the app could not have reached the gateway. A container in a compose network cannot reach a sibling through 127.0.0.1.
  • §13: only the primary can run archive_command. "Every Pod archives" is not a thing PostgreSQL does. The real protection is archive_timeout, plus the timeline history that lets a promoted replica find the missing head of the archive.
  • §13: the off-account dump was written into the on-account bucket path. That contradicted the tier table it sat next to.
  • §02: added who is allowed to talk to the DCS and the manager API. The mesh encrypts and admits; it does not authorise. Until this is closed, any enrolled device can rewrite your desired state.
  • §02: cluster move now says where the data comes from — the bucket, not a disk copy — and §10 says in what order etcd itself gets bootstrapped.
  • §13: replica creation and re-cloning come from R2, not from the primary. That is what makes "move a Pod to the home machine" and "the primary is down" survivable, and it takes load off the writer.
  • Failover timing is now one number, stated consistently (about forty seconds), and it matches the health-check settings that produce it.
  • §15 no longer asks questions; it records decisions. Control-plane auth, encryption mechanism, retention ordering, capacity limits, laptop enrolment, RTO/RPO objectives, failsafe_mode, secret delivery, upgrades and gateway fallback are all answered, each with the reasoning and the cost attached.
  • §14 and §11: the proposed fix for the DCS cliff was wrong. A fourth etcd voter gives quorum 3 and survives the same one host loss that three does. Surviving two losses takes five voters across five domains — so the cliff is documented, bounded, and the growth trigger is written down instead of a false improvement.
  • §12: the replica-build config used the wrong Patroni key and the wrong invocation. no_leader (not no_master on Patroni 4), a chained custom method with a manager-written script, because Patroni passes --datadir= rather than substituting a placeholder.
  • Cross-references fixed and abbreviations completed.

This section assumes you have never heard of any of this. It explains what you are building and why, lists everything you need before you begin, defines every word the rest of the document uses, and then walks the nine-step setup end to end — followed by the four command sets you will live in afterwards. If you read nothing else, read this.

What you are building, in plain words

You want a database that keeps working when a machine dies, a way to get back any moment you choose after a bad afternoon, and a way to create new ones without becoming a database administrator. This is what that looks like from the outside.

You have five computers: three that carry data, the one running your storefront, and one small box whose only job is to hold a vote. On each runs a small program called a manager. A manager does not serve your application and does not hold your data. It is a supervisor: it reads a list of what should be running, and it makes that true. That list lives in a shared database of its own — we call that the DCS, the Distributed Configuration Store — and every manager reads the same list, so they all agree without ever talking to each other. Five machines rather than three is deliberate: that shared store needs any three of the five alive, so losing two of them at once does not take your store read-only.

Three of those five carry the actual databases, called Pods. Three Pods, one per data machine, hold copies of the same data. One of them accepts writes. The other two follow along. That is replication, and it is the entire reason your site survives a dead machine: if the machine holding the writer dies, one of the others is promoted and takes over in about forty seconds.

Your application never talks to a Pod directly, because the address of the writer changes whenever a machine dies. Instead a small program called a gateway sits inside your application's own container setup and does the looking-up. Your code points at the gateway by service name and is completely unaware. The gateway is a few hundred lines of configuration and holds no state of its own.

And separately from all of that: every change is copied to Cloudflare's object storage as it happens, a full copy is taken every night and tested by actually restoring it, and if you delete a table on Tuesday you can be serving from Wednesday-morning-you-just-before-it by lunchtime. Redundancy protects you from a dead machine. Backups protect you from a live one.

To create all of this you type one command into a terminal. You do not need a laptop plugged in afterwards. The program that ran the command is a control machine, and you can close it, switch it off, or throw it away. Nothing it did is stored on it.

Settings that are off by default and will waste your afternoon

Cloudflare Zero Trust, before anything works
  • "Allow all Cloudflare One traffic to reach enrolled devices." Without this, your machines enrol successfully and then cannot see each other at all.
  • Split Tunnels must route the network's address range. By default the client sends ordinary internet traffic around Cloudflare and excludes the private range. Your machines will be on the network and still unable to reach each other.
  • Assign a unique address to each device. Every participant needs its own private address. Without it they collide and connections land on the wrong machine.
  • The device profile must use MASQUE, not WireGuard. Hostname routing and high availability are documented as not working under WireGuard. MASQUE is the default; just do not change it.
  • Do not trust the documented MTU — measure it. Cloudflare documents 1381 for the overlay; the number that matters is the one your path actually sustains today, and Cloudflare notes that Docker does not adjust for WARP on its own. pgdbaas doctor probes the real path MTU between every pair of hosts at bootstrap and again on demand, and refuses to place a Pod on a host that fails.

The words this document uses

Five of these are words this project invents, because there is no standard term for them. The rest are standard and you will meet them in the documentation of everything underneath. Learning these now will make every other page readable.

WordWhat it means here
control machineAny computer on the network that you run the command-line tool from. Holds no state, needed at no point after setup, safe to delete. Replaces the misleading phrase "stateless laptop".
managerOne long-running container per machine whose only job is supervision. It reads what should be running and makes it so. It holds no data, has no special role, and never votes on anything.
PodOne PostgreSQL database plus the program supervising it, with its own disk and its own address on the private network. A manager may run several. Three Pods together form one cluster.
gatewayOne small container that sits beside your application and forwards its database traffic to whichever Pod is currently the writer. Holds no state. Replaces the word "router", which suggests something that makes routing decisions.
clusterOne logical database. Three Pods, three machines, one answer to every question. Several clusters can share the same machines.
the writerAlso called the primary or master in older documentation. The single Pod accepting writes. Exactly one exists at a time, and only because the shared store says so.
followerAlso called a replica or standby. An exact copy that is a few milliseconds behind and takes no writes. One of them is special: the synchronous standby is the machine your writes wait for. See §09.
the shared storeThe DCS. A tiny database holding only metadata. Never your data.
overlay / the networkThe private network your machines share over the internet. Traffic is encrypted end to end. Referred to by its address range in a few places.
WALWrite-Ahead Log. PostgreSQL's ordered record of every change, in numbered segments. Followers stream this to stay identical. It is what makes a backup restorable to any moment.
the archiveThe copy of every WAL segment, forever-ish, sitting in R2. Continuous archiving means the archive keeps up within seconds. PITR is only possible while the archive is unbroken.
base backupA full copy of the database's data files at one instant. Useless on its own; the starting point that a WAL replay walks forward from.
failoverThe automatic promotion of a follower to writer when the current writer's machine dies. About forty seconds end to end, no human involved. The arithmetic is in §04 and the lock it depends on is in §08.
PITRPoint-In-Time Recovery. Getting the database back to a specific moment, not merely to the last backup. See §13.

Only three machines to start with? That works

Enrol what you have — three node adds and nothing else — and etcd forms with three members and quorum two. You then survive losing one machine, not two. Everything else in this quickstart is identical, including the gateway in your compose file: the only thing that changes between three machines and five is how many simultaneous losses you tolerate.

Growing to five later is a live operation with no downtime: node add the two extra hosts, then a membership change rather than a restart (the procedure and its trap are in §10). Do it before you need it — the day two hosts are down is the wrong day to be reading about quorum.

If you only remember three things

One. The only election in the system is which Pod is the writer, and it is settled by a shared store, not by the managers. Two. Your application talks to a gateway by service name and never learns a Pod's address, so failover is invisible to it — except that every open connection is dropped, so your connection pool's retry settings matter more than anything else in this document. Three. A write is confirmed to you only after it exists on two machines, and every change is copied to object storage within seconds — so losing a machine cannot lose an order you were told was saved, and losing a Tuesday can still be fixed on Wednesday.

Command sets  /  the four things you actually do

The whole CLI surface, grouped by the job you are doing rather than by the component it touches. Each command is argued for later in the document; this is the front end, in the order you will reach for it. The common thread: anything that reads (status, doctor, backup list, setup status) reads facts, so it works when things are broken; anything that writes is either reversible, or it makes you argue with it first.

Set 1 — running clusters

# ---- create ---------------------------------------------------------------
pgdbaas cluster create ecom-db --nodes n1,n2,n3 --pg 16
pgdbaas cluster create blog-db  --nodes n2,n3,n1 --pg 16   # same hosts, new Pods, new ports
                                                         # placement is explicit, never invented

# ---- look -----------------------------------------------------------------
pgdbaas status                    # one screen: nodes, clusters, roles, lag, backup health
pgdbaas topology ecom-db          # primary, sync standby, which Pod carries nosync, ports, lag
pgdbaas psql ecom-db              # a shell on whatever is primary right now

# ---- change, planned ------------------------------------------------------
pgdbaas switchover ecom-db --to ecom-2     # hand the primary over; no acknowledged write lost
pgdbaas cluster set ecom-db backup.retention=30d
pgdbaas cluster move ecom-2 --to n2        # rebuilds from R2. prints size and ETA before it starts
pgdbaas node drain n3                      # take a host out for maintenance, safely
pgdbaas node add --host 10.0.0.7 --user deploy --id n4

# ---- change, unplanned ----------------------------------------------------
pgdbaas failover ecom-db --force --to ecom-2   # promotes an async survivor; prints what may be lost

# ---- retire ---------------------------------------------------------------
pgdbaas cluster delete ecom-db       # refuses while a gateway still points at it

Set 2 — backups

# ---- what exists ----------------------------------------------------------
pgdbaas backup list ecom-db        # objects, sizes, ages - reads the bucket, not the fleet
pgdbaas backup history ecom-db     # every restore point inside the PITR window
pgdbaas backup now ecom-db         # take one right now

# ---- is it real -----------------------------------------------------------
pgdbaas backup verify ecom-db      # archive walk + a restore into a scratch Pod
pgdbaas backup repair ecom-db      # rotated credential, bucket refusing writes, wrong Pod env

# ---- keep it --------------------------------------------------------------
pgdbaas backup prune ecom-db       # dry run by default: prints the plan, deletes nothing
pgdbaas backup prune ecom-db --confirm
pgdbaas cluster set ecom-db backup.retention=30d backup.schedule="0 3 * * *"
pgdbaas tier3 enable --provider b2 # the third copy; `tier3 decline` records a permanent no

Set 3 — point-in-time recovery

# ---- 1. find the moment ---------------------------------------------------
pgdbaas backup history ecom-db          # what you can land on
pgdbaas status                          # how far back the window reaches

# ---- 2. recover to a NEW cluster: the default, destroys nothing -----------
pgdbaas restore ecom-db --to "1 hour ago" --as ecom-recovered
pgdbaas status ecom-recovered           # healthy? row counts right?

# ---- 3. repoint the app, or don't ----------------------------------------
pgdbaas gateway point ecom-recovered    # app reconnects onto the recovered copy
pgdbaas gateway point ecom-db           # ...and back again, if you didn't like what you saw

# ---- 4. clean up only when you are sure ----------------------------------
pgdbaas cluster delete ecom-recovered   # backups survive; --purge-backups is a separate argument

# ---- in a fire: over the live cluster, and it makes you argue ------------
pgdbaas restore ecom-db --to "15:32 UTC" --in-place --i-mean-it   # then type the cluster name

# ---- rehearse it, because an untested restore is a hypothesis ------------
pgdbaas verify ecom-db --full           # picks a random instant in the window and really recovers

Set 4 — seeing what you have

pgdbaas setup                 # the nine steps: resumable, safe to re-run
pgdbaas setup status          # what is configured, what is missing, and the command to fix it
pgdbaas setup step 9          # run any single step, out of order where that is allowed

pgdbaas status                # the one screen: nodes, clusters, roles, lag, backup health
pgdbaas doctor                # mtu, clock skew, free ports, quorum, storage, Zero Trust settings
pgdbaas topology ecom-db      # roles, sync standby, nosync, per-Pod ports and lag

pgdbaas config list ecom-db           # every effective setting, and where it came from
pgdbaas config get ecom-db backup.retention
pgdbaas gateway emit ecom-db          # regenerate the app's env file after any of the above
What divides these four sets

Sets 1–3 are about keeping data alive: create it, copy it, get it back. Set 4 is about never having to remember. Nothing in any of them requires the control machine to be running in order for a database to keep serving, and every read-only command is designed to still answer when the fleet is half dead — because that is the moment you run it.

Abbreviations

Every abbreviation in this document, expanded once.

DCSDistributed Configuration Store. A tiny shared key-value store — here etcd — holding only metadata: who is primary, cluster settings, member list, desired state. Never your data.
WALWrite-Ahead Log. PostgreSQL's ordered record of every change, in 16 MB segments. Replicas stream it to stay identical; the archive is its off-machine copy.
RTTRound-Trip Time. How long a request takes to reach the far side and come back. ~1-2 ms between your two VPS, ~25-30 ms to your home machine.
MTUMaximum Transmission Unit. Largest packet a link carries without splitting. Documented at 1381 for the overlay; a Docker bridge defaults to 1500. Mismatch is silent, so it is measured, not assumed.

01  /  The four things

Two corrections to the vocabulary before anything else, because the old words were doing damage.

Terminology

Not "stateless laptop." There is no such thing as a stateless machine. What you have is a control machine: a computer on the mesh that runs one CLI. It holds no state, is never needed at runtime, and nobody has to know it is online. Deleting it loses nothing. That is the property that matters, and it is a property of the design, not of a magical machine.

Not "router." The thing that fronts your database is a gateway, and it lives inside your application's own compose file, next to Medusa. Not on its own host, not holding a Pod. It is one more service in the stack you already run.

Control machine — where the CLI lives

A laptop, a workstation, a throwaway VM. It runs pgdbaas and it SSHes into your three hosts to bootstrap them. After bootstrap it can be closed, powered off, and forgotten. It comes back when you want to add a node, inspect a cluster, or destroy one. It is not a daemon and it is not in the data path.

One consequence worth naming early: the CLI is the only thing that writes desired state, and it does that over the mesh to the DCS. So it needs a client certificate (see §02) and a copy of the bucket credentials for backup list and restore. Both live in ~/.pgdbaas/ on the machine you ran node add from. Lose that directory and you lose the ability to restore from a clean laptop, which is exactly the moment you need it — so the bootstrap prints a one-time recovery bundle to keep in a password manager alongside the encryption passphrase.

Manager — one per host, permanently

A container on each machine whose only job is supervision: read what should be running, make it so, keep it so. Managers hold no cluster state, have no leader, and never coordinate with each other about who is in charge. A manager that dies takes its Pods with it and nothing else. A manager starting on a new host is a cold, empty container that converges.

Pod — spawned by a manager, many per manager

Patroni supervising one PostgreSQL instance, with its own data volume and its own DCS scope. Pods are disposable. A Pod's peers are the other Pods of the same logical cluster, wherever they live. A Pod that needs to be rebuilt pulls its starting point from object storage, not from its peers — which is why losing the writer does not stop a rebuild. See §12.

Gateway — one per application, inside your compose file

A small container beside your app that answers "which Pod is the primary of ecom-db right now?" and forwards TCP there. It holds no state. Your app points DATABASE_URL at the gateway's service name and never learns a single Pod address, never sees a failover, and needs no changes when the primary moves.

The load-bearing idea, restated

The only election in the system is per-Pod, inside Patroni, namespaced in the DCS. /service/ecom-db/leader and /service/billing-db/leader are unrelated keys. Everything else — managers, gateways, your control machine — has no vote and no lock.

02  /  Who tells the managers to create the Pods

"Manager A creates the Pod" does not create a cluster. Something has to tell Manager B and C that they also owe the system a Pod, and it cannot be a conversation between managers — that is a quorum problem wearing a trench coat.

The answer is the one Kubernetes arrived at twenty years ago, minus Kubernetes: the DCS holds the desired state, and every manager is an independent reconciler that only acts on its own slice. There is no message, no leader, no coordination. Each manager polls a key, decides what it owns, and makes it true.

Why the lease is not optional

A node-id is written to disk at bootstrap and never changes. If you reimage a host, reinstall, or restore a backup, you can end up with a second manager presenting the same node-id. Without a lease, both would read the same spec entry, both would start a Pod named ecom-0, and both would mount the same directory. That is a corrupt data volume, discovered later. So: acquire /pgdbaas/v1/nodes/<id>/lease with a 15-second TTL before doing anything, renew it every 5 seconds, and exit immediately if you cannot get it. It costs a few lines and it is the difference between a fleet and a hazard.

What this deliberately gives up

There is no automatic rescheduling. If ecom-2's host dies, the CLI or a human moves it by editing the spec — you pgdbaas cluster move ecom-2 --to n1 and Manager n1 creates it. For three machines and a handful of databases that is the right trade: a human decides where data lives, and the system never surprises you by moving a volume onto a disk that is full.

What cluster move actually does to the data

Revision 4 listed the command and never said where the bytes come from, which is the part a reader needs, because it is the part that costs time. There is no shared filesystem here, so a Pod's data cannot follow it. Moving a Pod means rebuilding it: 

pgdbaas cluster move ecom-2 --to n1

  1. manager n1 creates a new Pod from the cluster's create_replica_methods:
       wal-g backup-fetch from R2, then stream the tail from the current primary.
       (the same path a brand-new replica takes - see section 12)
  2. the new Pod is a normal caught-up replica. prints its lag and the wall-clock time.
  3. once lag is zero for 60s, the spec is rewritten in one CAS: ecom-2 now names n1.
  4. the old Pod on n3 is stopped and its volume deleted, after the spec flips.
  5. if step 3 fails, nothing has been destroyed and the command says so.

Two consequences worth stating before anyone is surprised by them. First, move is a network-bound operation whose cost is your database size divided by your link: a 40 GB move onto the home machine is not a keystroke, so the command prints the estimate from the bucket's object size before it starts, and it refuses if the target host lacks the free space. Second, it does not need the source Pod to be alive — which is the whole point. When a host is gone, the replacement procedure is the same command, and it works precisely because the starting copy lives in R2 rather than on the machine you lost.

Who may talk to what

This is the paragraph revision 4 was missing, and it is load-bearing rather than decorative. Cloudflare Mesh gives you an encrypted path and an admission check — "is this device enrolled in my Zero Trust organisation". It gives you no authorisation at the protocol layer. Every listener described in this document is therefore currently open to every enrolled device, and one of those is etcd, which holds the desired state of every cluster you own.

The assumption to write on the wall

Until the row above etcd is closed, the security model of this entire system is: every device enrolled in your Zero Trust organisation is root. That is a defensible model for a personal fleet. It is not a defensible model the moment a laptop, a phone, or a contractor's machine is enrolled — and enrolling devices is the one action Cloudflare makes trivially easy. Either close it, or keep the enrolment list as tightly guarded as the database password, which is what it effectively is.

03  /  Physical topology

Three manager hosts, each carrying one Pod of ecom-db and one etcd member. The application host is a fourth machine that is not part of the data path at all — it only holds the gateway sidecar.

04  /  The gateway

It sits inside your Medusa compose file, you point your app at it, and it figures out the rest. The design question is only how it learns the member list, because everything after that is a health check. The container's other job — pooling your application's connections — does not read the member list at all, and is §12.

Nothing about it is decided once. There is no seed list of Pods and no baked-in address. The gateway runs a resync loop forever: every 30 seconds it asks the managers for the current member list, diffs it against what it has, and reloads if anything changed. Between resyncs it is kept correct by health checks alone. What it does need is the three manager addresses, because that is where it asks. That is the one list, it is three values, and the CLI writes it for you.

The important consequence: a failed resync is harmless. If every manager is unreachable, the gateway keeps routing correctly from what it last learned, because the health checks never needed a manager in the first place. It only means the gateway will not discover Pods that appear after it lost the managers — and a gateway being created while every manager happens to be down (mid-upgrade, say) will fail to start rather than start blind. That is a real, if narrow, coupling; it is listed in §14.

The one thing your application must do, and it is not optional

Every failover drops every connection the app had open, at roughly the moment a machine died. A pool that does not retry turns a forty-second database event into an indefinite 500 page. So, for Medusa / TypeORM and anything else using node-postgres: retry on connect with exponential backoff for at least sixty seconds, keep statement-level retries on, and set an idle timeout well under HAProxy's 30-minute server timeout so the pool, not the proxy, decides when a connection dies. Test it by pulling the plug — drill "The primary Pod" in §11 — and treat "the storefront came back by itself" as the pass condition. This single setting is the difference between "highly available" on the diagram and in production, and no amount of care in the rest of the system can compensate for ignoring it.

Payments: the database cannot make a retry safe

§09 is honest that strict synchronous mode is not atomic: a client can be told its transaction failed at the exact moment the commit was already durable on two machines. Your application will retry, because that is what applications do — and for a checkout, a retry that books two orders or captures two charges is the failure you genuinely cannot accept. No setting in this document can prevent it, because the database cannot tell that the two attempts were meant to be the same attempt.

So the contract, at the application layer: every checkout carries an idempotency key generated before the first attempt and reused on every retry (payment providers already speak this — pass the same key and the charge happens once). Order numbers come from a unique constraint rather than a sequence you read first. Retries are bounded, and when they run out the user sees "we're checking whether your order went through" rather than a second attempt.

Test it the same way you test everything else here: during a switchover or a verify drill, place an order twice with one key. If one payment produced two orders, the store is not ready for money — however good the replication underneath is.

Wiring it into your compose file

The version in revision 4 published the gateway on the host's loopback and told the app to reach it at 127.0.0.1. That cannot work: a container gets its own loopback interface, and -p 127.0.0.1:5432:5432 publishes on the host's loopback, which a sibling container on the compose network is not on. The app would have hung at startup connecting to itself. Both working shapes are below; pick one, and the emitted env file says which one you picked. 

# docker-compose.yml, alongside medusa
services:
  db-gateway:
    image: ghcr.io/you/pgdbaas-gateway:1
    environment:
      DB_CLUSTER: ecom-db
      # Three values total. Everything else - member addresses, ports,
      # roles - is resynced from the managers every 30 seconds.
      DB_MANAGERS: "100.96.0.11:7000,100.96.0.12:7000,100.96.0.13:7000"
      DB_RESYNC_SECONDS: "30"
      DB_TLS_CERT: /etc/pgdbaas/gateway.crt     # see "who may talk to what", section 02
    expose:
      - "5432"        # one port: /ecom for writes, /ecom_ro for reads, both via db-gateway:5432
    restart: always   # the gateway is the app's only path to its data. Do not skip this.

  medusa:
    environment:
      DATABASE_URL: postgres://ecom:***@db-gateway:5432/ecom      # writes, and anything read-your-writes
      # catalogue reads go to the SAME host and port, only the name changes:
      #   postgres://ecom:***@db-gateway:5432/ecom_ro
      # (TypeORM: replicas: [ { url: ...ecom_ro } ]) - see "Reads" below
    depends_on: [db-gateway]
Reads: split by database name, not by a second port

/ecom_ro is the same database through the read pool: PgBouncer forwards it to HAProxy's read frontend, which counts only replicas currently passing Patroni's lag check. So catalogue, search and listing traffic can move off the primary with no query changes — one connection string. That is real headroom during exactly the event you care about: a traffic spike, when the primary's connection count and WAL output are both at their peak.

What it does not give you: read-your-writes. Even though the synchronous standby has flushed every commit, it may not have replayed it, and the read pool tolerates the byte lag in §12's config. Anything a user must see immediately after writing — an order confirmation, an account page, checkout — stays on /ecom. The split is a load decision, not a consistency feature, and a page that says "we could not find your order" two seconds after paying is a worse outcome than a slightly slower primary.

Two valid shapes — mixing them is the bug
  • App in the same compose file (the shape above): the gateway needs no ports: at all. expose: plus the compose network's DNS is the whole wiring, and DATABASE_URL names the service. Nothing is published on the host, so nothing is reachable from outside the app's own network.
  • App running on the host, not in a container: give the gateway network_mode: host so it can bind the host's loopback, and point the app at 127.0.0.1:5432. In this shape the gateway shares the host's network namespace, so the port-collision rules in §07 apply to it too — and 5432 is free precisely because app hosts and manager hosts are different machines.
  • Never publish 0.0.0.0:5432 in either shape. That puts your database on the public internet behind nothing but a database password, and this machine has a public IP.
The only configuration is which cluster, and where the managers are

Member addresses, ports, roles — all of that comes from the managers on a loop. Move a Pod to a different host or a different port and the gateway picks it up within 30 seconds without you editing anything. That is the difference between a config file that is true on the day you write it and a config file that is quietly wrong by next month. The manager addresses themselves are the exception, and they are as stable as your mesh assignments: three values, emitted once, regenerated by gateway emit.

05  /  Networking: why host mode solves it

The MTU problem is real, and the instinct to reach for a different container runtime was the right instinct for the wrong reason. Switching to Podman would not have fixed it. The problem is not the runtime. It is that a container on a private bridge network has an MTU the overlay path cannot carry, and every container runtime inherits that.

The actual fix is to not have a bridge. With --network host the container is in the host's network namespace: no veth pair, no docker0, no DNAT, no masquerade. It binds the host's addresses directly, including the Mesh address Cloudflare assigned.

What host networking does not break

The container still gets its own mount, PID, UTS, IPC and cgroup namespaces, its own root filesystem, and the default seccomp profile. It cannot see host processes or read host files it was not given. You are giving up one namespace, not six. Write network_mode: host out explicitly in the compose file so a reviewer can see exactly what was traded.

What this costs you, and how it is contained

Host networking pushes you toward a rootful Docker daemon, which means the daemon runs as root. Your instinct to avoid a root login was reasonable, so here is how the root is contained without you editing anything by hand.

One overstatement to correct first: host networking does not strictly require a rootful daemon. Rootless Docker supports --network host, and every port in this design is above 1024, so nothing needs privilege in order to bind. The reason this design still runs rootful is practical: the WARP connector is a system service, and the manager mounts the Docker socket and host paths for Pod volumes. Rootless-in-rootless networking adds a user-namespace layer whose interaction with a mesh interface nobody has measured. What rootless is good for here is as an escape hatch, and --userns-remap on the rootful daemon is a one-line bootstrap option that reduces what a container escape can reach. Say the trade plainly rather than claiming a constraint that is not one.

The bootstrap script — the one you copy to each machine and run once, described in §10 — does all of it: it runs as root for a minute, and it leaves behind a control machine that is not root and has no shell. 

# Written by pgdbaas-node.sh. You never type this.
restrict,command="/usr/local/bin/pgdbaas-remote" ssh-ed25519 AAAA... pgdbaas-control

# The control machine can only say:
#   "enroll this host as node n2 and start the manager."
# It cannot open a shell, cannot forward ports, cannot become root.
# The script prints this line when it writes it, so you can see it happened.

So the sequence is: you read a short script, run it once as root on each host, and from then on the control machine is confined to a single fixed command on each box. That is a narrower control surface than a root shell, and the only thing you had to do was run one file.

One INSERT from your app, tracing every layer. In host mode there is one fewer hop than a manager-port-forward design would have: the Pod binds the host's Mesh address directly.

07  /  The replication path and the port budget

Replication is Pod-to-Pod, peer to peer, and it never touches the gateway or any manager. In host mode it is a direct connection from one host's Mesh address to another's, with the real source IP intact.

Ports: a reserved range, not a proxy

Host networking means every port a Pod binds is a port on the host, so two Pods cannot share one. The requirement is: never hardcode a port, always know which ones are free, and be able to change the scheme later without redesigning anything.

Reserve a range. Pick a block nothing standard uses — 21000-21999 — verify it at bootstrap, and record the base in the DCS. Pod k takes two ports from it: one for PostgreSQL, one for the Patroni REST API. There is no proxy and no port demultiplexer, so replication stays a single direct hop and the source address survives.

Why a reserved range beat the fixed proxy window

A fixed-size window behind a manager-side demultiplexer sounds tidier — a small, enumerable port surface, immune to whatever else is on the host. It costs you two extra local TCP hops on every replication link, and it rewrites the source address, so pg_hba.conf could no longer name a host and your PostgreSQL logs could no longer tell you which machine connected. It also imposes a hard ceiling of Pods per host.

Reserving a range costs none of that. The surface is still fully enumerable — it is printed by pgdbaas status and stored in the DCS — and moving to a different range later is one value, not a redesign. In a three-host setup the window's main advantage was never worth those costs.

The one real cost, and its nastier variant

A service you did not control can grab a port in the range after bootstrap. Re-checking the specific pair immediately before every Pod create turns that from a mystery into an error message naming the port. Note the failure is asymmetric: a squatter bound to 0.0.0.0 makes the Pod fail to start, which is loud; one bound only to 127.0.0.1 lets the Pod come up on the mesh address while loopback traffic silently goes somewhere else, which is not. Check the pair against every bind address on the host, not just against the mesh one.

08  /  Leadership, demotion, and split brain

This is the mechanism that stops two Pods writing at once. It is the least intuitive part of the system, so here is all of it without jargon first.

The problem

Patroni keeps a leader lock in the DCS: the key /service/ecom-db/leader holds the name of the current primary, with a 30-second expiry. The primary renews it once per loop_wait, which is ten seconds by default.

Now imagine the network between your hosts breaks. The primary can no longer reach the DCS, nor the other two Pods. From where it sits, it cannot tell the difference between:

  • "the shared store is down" — in which case it is probably still alone, and probably fine; and
  • "I have been cut off from everyone" — in which case the other two Pods may already have promoted a different primary, and both halves now believe they are the writer.

That second state is split brain: two Pods both writing to the same dataset. It corrupts the data, and afterwards you cannot determine which of the two was right. Both are confidently wrong and neither can prove it.

The answer

When the primary cannot renew its lock, it demotes itself: it stops accepting writes and goes read-only. The heading says "demotion, not a partition heal" because Patroni does not attempt to repair the broken network. It cannot, from one side of a partition. It just refuses to be the writer without proof, and lets the other half sort itself out — where a healthy quorum will already have elected a replacement, and the demoted node will come back and rejoin as a replica.

This is a trade, and the trade is deliberate

Availability traded for safety. A flaky network gives you an outage instead of data loss. The cluster goes read-only for up to thirty seconds, then either recovers on its own or a human forces a promotion knowing exactly what might be lost. The DCS exists for precisely this one reason: to make the decision possible.

Split-brain prevention is a demotion, not a partition heal

Patroni cannot tell "the DCS is down" from "I am partitioned away." It assumes the worst and demotes PostgreSQL to read-only the moment it cannot refresh the leader lock — a retry_timeout-graceful, ttl-worst-case shutdown.

This is the good news: a flaky overlay causes an outage, not data loss, and it takes every cluster sharing that DCS at once. Patroni's failsafe_mode relaxes it — the primary re-confirms itself against every known member over its REST port instead of trusting the DCS alone. Turn it on, but read its sharp edges first: it only covers some DCS error paths, and an etcdserver: no leader error has been reported to bypass it and force a demotion anyway. It also raises a question this design has to answer on purpose rather than by accident — see the mesh drill in §11.

09  /  Synchronous replication

This is the decision that shapes everything else, and for an e-commerce store it is the right one. Here is exactly what it buys, what it costs, and the one tag that makes it cheap.

What "synchronous" actually means

A normal commit is: write the change locally, tell the client "OK", done. With synchronous_mode: on it becomes three steps:

  1. Write the change locally.
  2. Wait until at least one other Pod has received and flushed that change to its own disk.
  3. Only then tell the client "OK".

So when your app receives success, the change physically exists on two machines. If the writer's node dies one millisecond later, the other machine has the order. That is the entire guarantee, and it is exactly the failure you said you could not accept — a customer charged, and no order record.

The thing that is easy to misunderstand

Synchronous mode does not mean every standby is identical. At three Pods you have:

  • one writer (the primary),
  • one synchronous standby that is never behind — every commit is flushed to it before you are told "OK",
  • one asynchronous standby that may lag slightly, and is a spare.

Patroni maintains the synchronous role, so that standby is never the lagging one. The Pod that lags is the spare, and it is only ever touched when nothing better is available. So the shape you described — one writer, a reader that might be a touch behind, and the third being fine because it is a spare — is exactly what strict synchronous replication gives you. You get the shape and the guarantee.

One consequence people find surprising on a three-node quorum: losing the single synchronous standby can stall writes even though the primary is alive and a third Pod is healthy, because the third is tagged nosync and cannot take the role. Patroni reassigns within a loop, so this is a few-second hiccup rather than an outage, but it is the specific reason nosync deserves the explanation below rather than a comment in a YAML file.

Your hosts are not equidistant, and that matters

Step 2 above is a network round trip, so it lands directly on your write latency. And your three machines are not evenly placed:

  • Oracle VPS A to Oracle VPS B: ~1-2 ms. Same region, same private network.
  • Either VPS to your home machine: ~25-30 ms. Over the public internet, encrypted.

Patroni chooses the synchronous standby from the members that are eligible and least far behind in the WAL, not by measuring who is nearest. It has no idea your home machine is the slow one on the network, so it may well pick it. Tagging the far Pod nosync: true removes it from eligibility and forces synchronous replication onto the near one. That tag is not an optimisation toggle; it is a physical fact about your fleet, written down where the software cannot guess it.

The same guarantee, about fifteen times less waiting

nosync changes nothing about durability. It changes only which network your writes wait on. For a catalogue you would not notice either version; for checkout, 2 ms instead of 30 ms is the difference between a snappy store and one that feels like it is thinking.

Crucially, nosync does not exclude a Pod from elections. It is still a valid failover target. It is only skipped when choosing who to wait on. And if the near standby dies, Patroni reassigns the synchronous role to the far Pod automatically, so writes stay safe — just slower for as long as that lasts. 

# patroni.yml on the Pod that should never be the synchronous standby
tags:
  nosync: true

# Nothing else changes. If you ever move both VPS to one machine and the
# home node to the other, flip which Pod carries the tag. One line.
# doctor measures the RTT matrix between hosts and warns when this tag sits on
# the wrong Pod, or when fastest and slowest are within 10ms and it buys nothing.

The curve: 3 → 2 → 1

The rule underneath all of it: a write is only allowed if a copy lands on a second machine before you are told "OK".

The three behaviours that surprise people, stated plainly

1. The cluster can sit read-only with a live Pod. If the writer dies and the only survivor is an asynchronous replica, Patroni will not promote it automatically — because it cannot prove that Pod has every committed write. So you get a running Pod that is refusing to write, and nothing appears broken. This is the design working: the alternative is promoting a Pod that silently dropped your last orders. When you want it anyway, pgdbaas failover ecom-db --force --to ecom-2 drives Patroni's manual failover endpoint, which deliberately skips the lag and /sync-member checks, and it prints what may be lost before it does it.

2. Strict mode is not atomic, and one config value closes most of the gap. If a backend is cancelled while waiting for the replication acknowledgement — a client-side statement timeout, say — the commit can already be durable locally while your app is told it failed. The practical fix is boring: keep every client statement_timeout comfortably above your worst-case commit time. With the nosync tag, that means above about 30 ms; with the near standby dead and the role moved to the home node, above about 60 ms under load. So 5 seconds is a safe choice and 25 milliseconds is a bug waiting for a slow evening.

3. maximum_lag_on_failover defaults to one megabyte, and your fleet will exceed it. It is the ceiling a replica may be behind by and still be promoted, and it also bounds /replica health checks. Across a 30 ms residential link during a busy hour that is easy to blow through, and the symptom is a cluster that declines to fail over at exactly the wrong moment. Set it deliberately — tens of megabytes is the usual range for a WAN-attached standby — and read it back in pgdbaas status rather than trusting a default nobody chose. The same reasoning applies to maximum_lag_on_syncnode, which is how Patroni avoids choosing a laggy synchronous standby in the first place.

If you ever want reads from the synchronous standby to be guaranteed current rather than merely flush-current, set synchronous_commit: remote_apply. It makes the primary wait until the standby has replayed the change, not just received it. More latency, stronger read guarantee. The default on is right for your case.

The row people skip: two Pods

Two Pods is not "half degraded". It is the safety net is gone. Writes still work and are still safe, but there is no spare: a writer failure is now a genuine outage until a node comes back, rather than a forty-second blip. Worth knowing before you are one node down and surprised.

10  /  Bootstrapping with one script

The requirement is one command that takes a list of hosts and ends with a working three-node cluster, without hand-editing authorized_keys or any other config on five machines. The shape is: a single script you copy to each machine and run once. 

# on each of the three hosts, as root:
scp pgdbaas-node.sh you@10.0.0.4:/tmp/
ssh you@10.0.0.4 'sudo /tmp/pgdbaas-node.sh --id n1'

# That is the whole install. The script:
#   1. prints every action it is about to take, and waits for you
#   2. installs cloudflare-warp and enrols this host as a Mesh node
#   3. installs and configures Docker
#   4. scans the reserved port range and reports anything already taken
#   5. writes /etc/pgdbaas/node.json  { id, mesh_ip }
#   6. writes the node's client certificate for the DCS and the manager API (section 02)
#   7. installs the manager and its control socket
#   8. adds the control machine's key, restricted to one fixed command
#   9. is idempotent - safe to re-run after a failure or a reinstall
#
# Note what the script does NOT do: it does not start etcd and it does not create
# a cluster. The control machine does both, and in a specific order, below.

Then the control machine does the rest:

  1. Pin by node-id, not by IP. The script writes the id to disk at first boot and the spec keys off that, so DHCP or a changed address does not reshuffle your cluster.
  2. Install the SSH restriction as part of the script, not by hand. The control machine can only ever run one fixed command on a host — never open a shell, never forward a port. You never touch this file; the script writes it and shows you the line it wrote.
  3. Verify before you trust. After install, the control machine checks the Mesh route, the measured path MTU, clock skew, and the free port pairs, and refuses to continue with the name of the check that failed.

The order etcd comes up in, because getting it wrong is unrecoverable

Every previous revision said "the DCS runs on the machines" and skipped the part where five processes that need each other in order to start are started on five machines that may not all be there yet. etcd has two bootstrap modes and only one of them is safe to leave configured. 

pgdbaas node add n1,n2,n3,n4,n5      # phase A - nothing is started yet
  # CLI collects {name, peer-url} for all five and writes an identical
  # initial-cluster block into each machine's etcd config:
  #   n1=https://100.96.0.11:2380,n2=https://100.96.0.12:2380,n3=https://100.96.0.13:2380
  #   ,n4=https://100.96.0.14:2380,n5=https://100.96.0.15:2380
  # with initial-cluster-state: new, plus each node's client/peer certificates.

pgdbaas node add --start              # phase B - members start, form raft, elect a leader
  # waits for health from all five, then FLIPS THE CONFIG:
  #   initial-cluster: empty,  initial-cluster-state: existing
  # That flip matters. Leaving a populated initial-cluster behind means that a
  # later restart of a single member after a long outage tries to re-bootstrap a
  # fresh cluster instead of rejoining, and etcd refuses to start with
  # "member already exists" - on the one component you cannot recreate.

pgdbaas cluster create ecom-db        # phase C - only now is a spec written
  # refuses to proceed unless all five members are healthy and the three selected nodes hold their leases

Growing the fleet later uses membership, never initial-cluster: node add n4 runs etcdctl member add against the live cluster first, then starts the fourth process pointed at the existing peers. That is also exactly the procedure for the fourth, always-on vote proposed in §14 to decouple the DCS from managers — which is why that change is small.

Two more rules, both cheap to honour and expensive to retrofit. The etcd data directory lives on its own volume and never shares one with a Pod: an etcd member running out of disk mid-fsync is a corruption risk that takes every cluster down, and a Pod restoring 40 GB from R2 is precisely the thing that would do it. And --max-snapshots retention plus a compaction and defragment timer are set from the start, because etcd grows silently and the failure it produces — "mvcc: database space exceeded" — freezes the control plane of a system that is otherwise healthy.

On the trust question, honestly

A restricted key means the remote side can only do one thing. A script you run means you trust the whole script. That is a real difference, and the mitigation is not clever — it is that the script is short, it lives in this repository, and step 1 makes it print what it is about to do. Read it once, run it, and the control surface is contained.

Design these as tests. Each has a specific correct behaviour, and the ones flagged are where this design will actually surprise you. Every timing here is the same arithmetic as §04 and §09 — if you change a timeout in one place, three tables in this document need re-deriving.

Kill thisExpectedWhere it goes wrong
The primary Pod Leader lock TTL (30s) expires, Patroni promotes the synchronous standby — which by construction has every committed write. PostgreSQL restarts as primary, and about six seconds later (rise 2 at inter 2s) the gateway agrees. End to end about forty seconds. No acknowledged order is lost, which is the entire point of §09. Two things, and they are in your application, not here. HAProxy must drop sessions on mark-down, not just stop routing new ones. And a pool with no connect-retry turns a forty-second event into a down storefront — see §04.
The standby, primary alive Patroni reassigns the synchronous role to the remaining replica and writes keep flowing. If that replica is the home machine, write latency temporarily becomes ~30ms until the near node returns. A client statement_timeout set below the new commit time. Transactions start failing on a healthy cluster — sharp edge 2 in §09.
One host Two Pods left, still reading and writing (see §09). The cluster loses its spare, so a writer failure is now a real outage rather than a blip. A new manager on a replacement host converges, and you rebuild the lost Pod wherever you want it with cluster move, from the bucket. Nothing automatic here by design. A human decides where data lives — which is also why move must print its size and ETA before it starts.
Two hosts etcd still holds quorum (3 of 5) and every cluster keeps writing. This is a degradation, not an outage — it was read-only before the fifth vote existed. You are using two of the three survivors. Nothing breaks, which is exactly why you must act: add a replacement host before the next failure, because the third one is the real boundary.
Three hosts etcd loses quorum, every cluster demotes to read-only within retry_timeout + ttl. Data is intact; writes are not available. This is the availability cliff. Rebuild hosts, then rebuild Pods out of R2 — a restore, not a repair. The cliff is real and no voter count within five machines moves it: see the DCS row in §14.
The gateway The app's connections fail. PgBouncer's reserve pool absorbs the reconnect burst instead of forwarding it to PostgreSQL; restart: always brings the container back in seconds, and it rebuilds its whole view from the managers within one resync interval. The cluster is completely unaffected. A single gateway is a single point of failure for the application, however stateless it is. Run a second gateway, or run the gateway on the host's network namespace so a container restart cannot strand it. Without pool retry the outage looks like a database outage. It isn't.
The application host The cluster keeps writing: quorum drops from five voters to four and still holds, and the dead member rejoins as a normal member when the host returns. What you lose is the application itself, which is outside this project — nothing here runs the storefront. Worth testing so you trust it: every other row in this table protects data, and this is the one row that does not. The database's job here is finished when quorum holds.
Mesh for 60s Patroni in every cluster loses the DCS and its peers, primaries demote. Nothing corrupts, everything goes read-only, and it recovers on its own when the overlay returns. Decided: on. A primary that can still reach all of its own Pods keeps its lock, so a partial mesh failure — the failure a flaky home connection actually produces — costs you nothing. A total one costs the same as before: failsafe_mode relaxes some DCS error paths, not all of them.
One Pod's network, silently Patroni marks it down, gateways stop selecting it, it rejoins when the path returns. A TCP-checked proxy would keep sending writes to a partitioned primary — this is why the REST check is mandatory. Any component that health-checks the data port instead of the REST /primary endpoint will eventually write to a replica or a zombie. Including a hand-written health check you added "temporarily".
A Pod that was down comes back It rejoins as a replica — it is not promoted for having been down. If its WAL is a clean subset of the primary's it resumes streaming. If it diverged, Patroni rewinds it with pg_rewind or wipes and rebuilds it. Either way, once caught up it is a full candidate for future elections. The rebuild path matters: pulling a base backup from R2 rather than from the primary is what makes this survivable when the primary is also down, and it keeps a 40 GB rebuild off the writer. wal_log_hints: on is what makes the rewind path cheap instead of forcing the rebuild.
A host reboot Everything comes back. PostgreSQL is only ever started by Patroni. Anything that can also start PostgreSQL — a stray systemd unit, a compose restart policy on the data container, a leftover pg_ctl alias — is a split brain waiting to happen. There must be exactly one thing in the system that starts the database.
Reimage a host, keep the old id The new manager cannot take the node lease and exits with a clear error. You enrol it as a new node instead, or release the lease deliberately once you have confirmed the old manager is gone. Without the lease: two Pods named the same thing on one volume. This is the failure the lease in §02 exists to prevent, and the reason "just delete the stuck lease key" needs to be a deliberate command rather than an etcdctl invocation.
The R2 credentials, mid-write Archiving fails. PostgreSQL keeps running — archive_command failing does not block commits until WAL piles up — but the PITR window stops growing, and status goes amber then red. The quiet disaster. If nobody looks, WAL accumulates on the data volume until the disk fills and the database stops for a reason that looks nothing like a backup problem. pgdbaas doctor treats "archive failing" and "WAL directory growing" as one condition, and the fifteen-minute timer in §13 is what makes it loud.

12  /  Inside the gateway: pooling, then role selection

The gateway's entire job, and it is two processes in one container. PgBouncer faces your application on 5432 and multiplexes many client connections onto a handful of server ones — without it, a traffic spike or a failover reconnect storm exhausts PostgreSQL's connection limit. HAProxy faces the Pods, on loopback, and decides which of them is the writer. The app never talks to HAProxy; HAProxy never talks to the app. Ordering matters, and it is app → PgBouncer → HAProxy → Pod: role selection has to happen on every connect, while pooling is a client-facing concern.

This is the config the sidecar renders from the member list it resynced; what you see below is the output, and the template has three placeholders per server rather than three hand-maintained lines. It is the same config whether the gateway runs in your app's compose file or on a host of its own.

The revision 4 bug, spelled out, because it is easy to repeat

Revision 4 had three server lines that each health-checked port 21001. In HAProxy, check port N keeps the server's own address and overrides only the port — so all three probes went to each host's port 21001, which only exists on Pod 0's host. Pod 1's check went to 100.96.0.12:21001 (nothing listening; its REST port is 21003) and Pod 2's to 100.96.0.13:21001 (its REST port is 21005). Two of three servers would sit permanently DOWN, and in the topology every diagram in this document uses, the permanently-down one was the primary: the gateway would have had no healthy backend and refused every connection. The failure is silent at startup and total at runtime, which is a bad combination to discover on day one.

The fix is to let the port vary per server, which is what the k in §07 was always for. Use check port <base+2k+1>, or the explicit form check addr <mesh-ip> port <rest-port> when you want it spelled out. And since the list is generated, the correct defence is a rendering test: assert that each server line's check port is its own REST port, because this class of error passes every review that reads the config with its eyes instead of with a socket.

Why pooling is the production half of this section

PostgreSQL defaults to max_connections = 100, and Postgres is worse than almost anything else at handling a few hundred connections at once — every extra session costs memory and scheduling even when idle. Two events in this document generate connection spikes: a traffic spike, and a failover, when your application's entire pool reconnects within a second or two. Without a pooler, those are the two events most likely to take the store down while the database is perfectly healthy.

The one thing transaction pooling costs you: server connections are shared, so connection-scoped state leaks between clients. Anything your app sets with SET (including a session statement_timeout) lands on whichever client uses that connection next. Put timeouts in postgresql.conf, on a role, or on the database — not per session — and keep statement_timeout well above your worst-case commit time as §09 says.

Ordering is not negotiable: PgBouncer in front of HAProxy means a failover costs your existing server connections an error and then routes the reconnect to the new primary automatically. The reverse order would need a PgBouncer reload on every failover, in the exact seconds you can least afford one.

Why an HTTP check and not a TCP one — and one thing to avoid

A TCP check to the data port passes on the primary and on every replica, because a hot standby accepts connections. You would load-balance writes across the whole cluster and watch the app throw cannot execute INSERT in a read-only transaction after every failover. Patroni's REST API exists so a proxy can ask the question a socket cannot: are you the leader right now, and do you still hold the lock? Only /, /primary and /read-write return 200 for the primary with the lock held.

The thing to avoid is combining option httpchk with http-check send in the same proxy: they are the legacy and the current way of expressing the same request, and which one wins is not worth reasoning about at 3am. Pick one form per frontend. Above, the write frontend uses the legacy form because it needs nothing else, and the read frontend uses the current form because it needs a query string.

13  /  Backup and point-in-time recovery

Until this section existed, the system was highly available and completely unprotected — a bad migration or a dropped table would have taken the database with it, redundancy and all. This is the design that closes that gap, and it is the section the whole project is named after, so it is written to be operated by someone who is tired.

The tool, and why

WAL-G (a single static binary) writing to a Cloudflare R2 bucket. Three properties decided it:

  • No backup server. WAL-G talks to object storage directly. That means no extra machine to keep alive — which matters more than usual here, because another always-on node would be another vote in the DCS quorum and another thing that can take your clusters down.
  • Configuration is environment variables. The manager generates them per Pod. There is no config file for you to hand-edit, which was an explicit requirement.
  • It can prove its own backups are restorable. WAL-G ships archive and backup verification commands that walk what is actually in the bucket and report any missing segment — which is precisely the failure that silently turns "point-in-time recovery" into "I can only go back to Tuesday."

The R2 specifics are worth writing down because they are where S3-compatible setups usually fail on the first try: AWS_ENDPOINT points at the account's S3 API URL, WALG_S3_PREFIX is an s3://bucket/pgdbaas/… key, and AWS_S3_FORCE_PATH_STYLE=true is required — without it WAL-G tries a virtual-hosted-style hostname that R2 does not resolve, and the error you get is a DNS failure that looks nothing like a configuration mistake.

I looked hard at pgBackRest, which has the better feature story — page-level incrementals, parallel restore, and real repository verification. Two things ruled it out. It wants to co-locate with the database host or reach it over SSH, which is a poor fit for config this system generates. And in April 2026 its sole maintainer archived the repository; a coalition revived it within a week, so it is healthy now, but a backup tool is precisely the dependency where a single maintainer is a risk you are taking on other people's behalf. Barman needs a dedicated backup server, which defeats the first point above.

How the archive survives a failover — corrected

Revision 4 claimed "every Pod archives its own WAL, so a leader change cannot open a gap." That is not how PostgreSQL works, and a claim this load-bearing needs to be right rather than reassuring. Only the primary runs archive_command. A standby does not archive; it runs restore_command to pull. If you configure a standby to push, you have a bug, not a redundancy.

So the archive's continuity has to come from somewhere else, and it does — from four things working together, each of which is a setting rather than a hope:

  1. The promoted Pod archives what it is holding. Synchronous replication means the standby that gets promoted already has every acknowledged record on disk. On promotion it becomes the archiver and pushes the segments sitting in its own pg_wal — including the ones the dead primary never got around to. The archive stays complete for acknowledged data because replication made two copies, not because two machines were pushing.
  2. Replication slots stop the primary recycling WAL too early. With use_slots: true, each replica pins the WAL it still needs. Without slots, a busy primary can rotate a segment out of pg_wal before a lagging replica or the archive has it, and that is a genuine hole.
  3. archive_timeout: 60 bounds how stale the tail of the archive can look. Segment switches happen on volume, not on the clock; with the default of 0 a quiet database can leave a partly-filled segment unarchived for hours. That is what makes the status line "last WAL archived 40s ago" mean something, and it is what bounds the distance between the last recoverable instant and now.
  4. Timeline history files get archived too. A failover creates a new timeline, and PITR to a point after one needs the .history file. WAL-G archives them; a design that names only WAL segments loses the ability to recover past the first failover, which will be on an unmemorable night six months from now.
The slot that protects you is also the slot that can fill your disk

A replication slot pins WAL until the replica consumes it, so a replica whose host is off for a fortnight makes the primary accumulate WAL until the data volume is gone — and that stops the database, for a reason that looks nothing like a backup problem. Set max_slot_wal_keep_size to a budget you actually have. When a slot exceeds it, PostgreSQL invalidates the slot and that replica needs a full rebuild — which is a bad afternoon, but a full rebuild from R2 is a bad afternoon that ends, whereas a full disk on the primary is a bad afternoon that takes the storefront down. pgdbaas doctor reports pinned-WAL bytes per slot for exactly this reason.

pgdbaas backup list ecom-db         # what exists, how big, how old - straight from the bucket
pgdbaas backup prune ecom-db        # dry run. prints the plan, deletes nothing
Encryption: decided, and the mechanism is asymmetric on purpose

WAL-G's WALG_PGP_KEY, with the public key only in every Pod. A Pod can encrypt what it pushes and can never decrypt it. The private key is generated once, stored on the managers at /etc/pgdbaas/keys/, printed once with a passphrase for your password manager, and kept out of the DCS because the DCS is readable by every Pod in every cluster. If all three hosts are lost, the backups are still readable: by you, with that one key.

The alternatives were checked rather than assumed. WAL-G's libsodium backend needs a binary built with USE_LIBSODIUM=1 and puts a secret key on all three hosts — faster, but any host compromise reads the archive. R2 supports SSE-C, but WAL-G has no SSE-C variable, so server-side customer keys are not reachable from this tool (WALG_S3_SSE is AES256 or aws:kms); R2's own encryption at rest still applies, as belt and braces.

Verify it once, at bootstrap, and again in every drill: push one object, confirm the object is not plaintext without the key, pull it back with the private key. A build that silently lacks encryption would otherwise look exactly like a build that has it.

The one screen that answers "am I safe?"

This is the most valuable thing in the whole section. Most people discover their backups are broken at restore time, which is the worst possible moment. So backup health is a first-class line in pgdbaas status, and it goes red when it should: 

$ pgdbaas status

  ecom-db       running   primary ecom-1 (VPS B)   lag 0s
    last full backup     6h ago     full 03:00        ok
    last WAL archived   40s ago                       ok
    PITR window          14 days (since 15 Sep)        ok
    last verified        9h ago     restore drill     passed
    recoverable to       any moment in the last 14 days
    size in bucket       2.1 GB                        ~$0.03/mo
    off-account dump     7h ago     pg_dump 03:30       ok

  billing-db    running   primary billing-2 (home)   lag 0s
    last full backup     31h ago                       LATE
    last WAL archived   6h ago                        STUCK
    PITR window          4 days (since 25 Sep)      DEGRADED
    last verified        never                     NEVER RUN
    size in bucket       840 MB
    pinned WAL in slots  11 GB on ecom-0 - one slot not advancing      WARN

A stuck archive is the loud one. It means the window is closing right now, and it is the failure that turns into a disaster if you find out a week later. pgdbaas doctor names the cause, and pgdbaas backup repair ecom-db fixes the common ones: a rotated credential, a bucket that stopped accepting writes, a Pod that came up with the wrong env, a slot pinning WAL on a dead replica.

It has to answer even when the cluster cannot

The single most useful property of backup list is that it does not touch the fleet at all: it reads the bucket with the control machine's credentials. That is the difference between a backup tool and a backup tool that goes quiet on the one night you need it — the night the cluster is down is precisely the night a status command that asks the cluster for an answer learns nothing. So pgdbaas backup list and restore must work from a clean laptop with nothing but ~/.pgdbaas/ and a passphrase, and that path is exercised by the monthly drill rather than assumed.

Make it shout without a monitoring stack

You do not have Prometheus here, and you should not build one to find out your backups are broken. Put a systemd timer on the control machine: pgdbaas doctor --quiet every fifteen minutes, appending to a log, plus one line that mails you on failure if you want. And because the gateway already runs a health endpoint for your app, it can expose /backup-status — so an external uptime check covers this too. Note what that timer cannot do: if the control machine is off, nothing checks. A second timer on any manager, or the gateway's endpoint polled by a free external monitor, is what makes the alarm independent of your laptop being open.

A restore is a create, not a destroy

This is the single most important decision in the backup design, and it is a UX decision as much as a technical one.

The intuitive version of a restore — wipe the cluster, load the backup, start serving — means that if you pick the wrong timestamp, you have just destroyed the data you were trying to save. That is how a bad afternoon becomes a bad month.

So the default creates a new cluster alongside the old one. Nothing is destroyed until you deliberately destroy it: 

# what you will actually type, in the moment you need it
pgdbaas backup history ecom-db                  # see what exists, straight from the bucket
pgdbaas restore ecom-db --to "1 hour ago" --as ecom-recovered
# ...read the output, check the row counts, then:
pgdbaas gateway point ecom-recovered
# ...and days later, once you are sure:
pgdbaas cluster delete ecom-db

# the delete refuses while a gateway still points at the cluster,
# so you cannot lose a database to a typo in a name
Where the drill runs, because it needs a real disk

A verified restore is a full restore: it needs roughly the database's size in free space, and the restore-to-scratch after every full backup plus a monthly PITR drill plus a nightly pg_dump is a lot of I/O to put next to a production Pod. So the drill targets the manager with the most free disk, announces itself in the DCS so status can show it running, uses a throwaway volume, and is capped: verify.max_disk and verify.skip_when_free_below. If no host can host the drill, that is a red line in status rather than a silently skipped test — a verification that quietly stops running is the exact failure mode this section exists to prevent.

Redundancy, honestly

Three Pods on three hosts is not redundancy for a backup, because every one of those hosts depends on the same overlay, the same home connection, and the same credentials. So the backup gets its own layered answer, and it is worth being clear about which tiers are load-bearing and which are optional.

TierWhatSurvivesCost
1 — always on WAL-G → R2, your own account. Object-storage durability, offsite from home, no egress fees. Host loss, home connection loss, Pod loss, a bad migration, an accidental DROP. RPO measured in seconds. Roughly $0.015/GB-month. Negligible at catalogue scale.
2 — always on Nightly compressed pg_dump → a bucket in a second Cloudflare account, at a path that exists nowhere in the first. Deliberately a different tool and a different login. WAL-G breaking, the first account being locked out or compromised, a retention bug in tier 1, logical mistakes that a physical restore faithfully reproduces. RPO 24 hours. Negligible. One bucket and one scheduled job.
3 — optional Mirror the dump to a second provider. The only tier covering "Cloudflare is having a bad day at the exact moment you need a restore." A Cloudflare-wide outage, including the storage API being unreachable mid-recovery. Cheap storage, plus egress when you actually restore.
4 — not recommended A local copy on each of the three hosts. Nothing that matters. All three depend on the same mesh and roughly the same power supply. Wasted disk, and a false sense of safety.
What tiers 1 and 2 together actually buy you

Losing your Cloudflare account, your bucket, or your backup tooling: covered. Cloudflare having a regional outage while you are mid-restore: not covered without tier 3 — though the window is narrow, because a storage outage does not take your database down, only your ability to recover during it.

One gap neither tier closes, and revision 4's table claimed it did: silent corruption that lives inside the data rather than in the archive. If a bad page write is committed, the WAL faithfully records it, every backup contains it, and a restore drill that only checks "does it boot and do the row counts match" will report everything healthy. That is what step 4 of the drill is for — amcheck on a rotating index per schema, plus a stored digest of row counts for the handful of tables where a wrong number is an incident. It is not comprehensive and it should not be described as such; it is the cheap 80% and it is honest about being that.

I would start on tiers 1 and 2, and add tier 3 the first time you find yourself rehearsing a restore and notice you are holding your breath for a provider. That is a reasonable trigger, and it can be deferred.

The retention footgun, designed around

After a major PostgreSQL upgrade the internal timeline number resets, and ordering by name can decide your newest backups are old and delete them. That is a real documented behaviour, and it bites on exactly the week you upgraded.

Two fixes, and the document uses both. The path split above keeps two timelines out of one history. And WAL-G's own documentation names the remaining case: after pg_upgrade the timeline resets while older backups stay in the same storage, so ordering by name can decide your newest backups are old and delete them. Its answer is wal-g delete --use-sentinel-time, which orders by backup start time instead of by name. Every prune in this system passes it. Pruning is a dry run by default — WAL-G's default too, which is a rare case of a tool and a design agreeing — and the manager refuses to prune a history with fewer than two fulls.

What this does not cover

  • Recovery point. With continuous archiving plus synchronous replication it is zero for acknowledged writes and up to one archive_timeout for what the archive can show you. A restore is bounded below by your last full plus the WAL replay, and the monthly drill is what gives you the real number instead of the imagined one.
  • Recovery time. Proportional to database size and to the mesh. A restore pulling gigabytes from R2 down to your home machine is slower than the same restore to an OCI box. Measure it during the drill rather than discovering it.
  • Logical corruption beyond what amcheck samples. Backups faithfully preserve a dropped table; nothing in a physical backup design can tell you a row was deleted by mistake. Catching that needs dump diffs or digests on critical tables — a separate project.
  • A stolen passphrase. Encryption makes a leaked bucket useless, but whoever holds the passphrase can read everything. Treat it like the database password, because it is one.
  • A deleted bucket. Tier 1 and tier 2 both live in object storage, and a bucket can be deleted by someone with write access and a bad afternoon. Tier 3 is the only answer, which is the argument for eventually taking it.
The part that actually matters

Everything above is plumbing. What protects you is the automatic verification after every full backup, and a broken archive turning a line in pgdbaas status red instead of remaining a surprise. A backup you have never restored is a hypothesis, not a backup — and because R2 has no egress fees, testing that hypothesis costs a few minutes of disk and nothing else.

14  /  Lifecycle: the rest

Backups, replication, failover and the control plane are designed. What remains here is execution rather than design — the decisions themselves are recorded in §15, so this section is what is left to build. Each item is the kind of thing that only gets built once, badly, in production.

SurfaceWhat is decided, and what is left to build
Deletion Two-phase and hard to do by accident. pgdbaas cluster delete ecom-db refuses while any gateway still points at the cluster, because a cluster name is load-bearing in a live app's config. Then it asks you to type the name. Only after that does it destroy Pods and volumes — and backups only behind a separate flag plus its own typed confirmation, since the bucket is the only thing left after the fleet dies. Deleting an orphaned bucket still costs a decision, not a click: say how much history is in it and when it was last verified.
Secrets Database passwords cannot live in the DCS, because every Pod in every cluster can read it. Each Pod gets a generated credential the manager hands out once at creation. Decided: the superuser password is printed once into ~/.pgdbaas/credentials (0600) and to the terminal for your password manager, retrievable later only with a manager client certificate, rotated with cluster set db.password and never written to the DCS. The backup key follows the same rule and is asymmetric (§13). What is left to build is the delivery path and the file permissions.
Upgrades Decided: rolling, one Pod at a time, gated on two healthy Pods (cluster upgrade ecom-db --to 17, §15). Manager upgrades use the same drain: stop placing Pods on a host, take it out, upgrade, put it back. A major upgrade is also when the storage path in §13 changes, and the moment the restore path deserves retesting before you need it. The 3 → 2 state is exactly when an upgrade is safe and exactly when it feels least safe, so the command has to say which of those two facts it is acting on.
Decoupling the DCS Grown to five voters across five enrolled hosts; quorum three. The three data hosts, the app host, and one cheap VPS whose only job is holding a vote. Losing any two still leaves a writable cluster; three is the boundary, and three means restore from R2, not repair. The arithmetic is why five: majority quorum means four voters need three alive, so four tolerates the same single loss that three does — it changes which machine must die, not how many. Five needs three, which tolerates two. Note the correlated risk that makes this worth the money: both Oracle VPS are in one region, so account or region trouble costs two votes at once. Cost: one spare VPS (~$6/month) and enrolling the app host, which gains no Pods and no data.
Control-plane authorisation Decided: close it before the first cluster create (§15), designed in §02. What is left to build is the private CA and cert issuance at enrolment, etcd --client-cert-auth with three roles, mTLS on the manager API, restapi.authentication on Patroni's mutating endpoints, and scram-sha-256 on every pg_hba entry. If it is not built, the enrolment list is root access and must be governed as such.
Gateway coupling The gateway reads the member list from the manager API on port 7000, so a gateway created while all three managers are down cannot start. Decided: keep it that way (§15) — a seed fallback to the DCS would give the gateway two ways to fail. The window fails loud rather than blind, restart: always is mandatory, and gateway emit warns when you are running only one gateway.

15  /  Decisions that were open

These were questions. They are now decisions, with the reasoning attached, so that nobody has to re-derive them at 2am. Where a decision rests on a vendor behaviour, the behaviour is named rather than assumed.

QuestionThe decisionWhy this one, and what it costs
Control-plane authorisation Closed before the first cluster create, not after. A private CA lives only on the managers; every device gets a client certificate at enrolment; etcd runs --client-cert-auth with three RBAC roles (manager, Patroni scope, CLI); the manager API is mTLS; Patroni's mutating endpoints sit behind restapi.authentication; every pg_hba entry is scram-sha-256. Patroni documents basic auth as protecting the unsafe endpoints while the health-check endpoints stay open without TLS — so an enrolled device can read role state but cannot demote your primary, which is the trade we want. Retrofitting etcd client-cert auth is a rolling restart (2 of 3 keeps quorum), but doing it later means coordinating the one component everything else waits on. Cost: one CA, one cert per device, and the discipline to renew them.
Backup encryption WAL-G WALG_PGP_KEY, asymmetric. Pods receive the public key and can only encrypt on push; the private key exists on the managers and in your password manager, nowhere else. WAL-G's libsodium backend needs a build with USE_LIBSODIUM=1 and would put a secret key on all three hosts; PGP works in the stock release binary and means a compromised Pod or a leaked bucket still cannot read your backups. R2 encrypts at rest with Cloudflare-managed keys regardless, and R2 does support SSE-C — but WAL-G exposes no SSE-C variable (WALG_S3_SSE is AES256/aws:kms only), so server-side keys are belt and braces, not the promise. Verified by a round-trip at bootstrap and by every drill.
Retention ordering Both fixes, always. One storage path per major version, and every wal-g delete runs with --use-sentinel-time. Prune stays a dry run unless confirmed. WAL-G documents the footgun directly: after pg_upgrade the timeline resets while older backups remain, so name order can delete the newest backup, and --use-sentinel-time orders by backup start time instead. A per-version path hides the problem; the flag removes it. Neither costs anything, so doing only one of them is a decision to keep a known bug.
How a new Pod gets its data A custom Patroni replica method, wal_g_r2, with no_leader: 1, falling back to basebackup. A small manager-written script runs wal-g backup-fetch from the newest backup, then streams the tail from whatever leader exists — possibly none. Patroni chains methods until one returns 0, and no_leader is what permits building a replica with no reachable leader at all (it is spelled no_master before Patroni 4). This is what makes cluster move and "the primary is down" survivable, and it keeps a rebuild off the writer. Cost: a rebuild is network-bound, so it is rate-limited (next row).
Large rebuilds vs. replication Clone traffic is rate-limited by default (WALG_NETWORK_RATE_LIMIT, half the link), with --full-speed to opt out. Above 150 GB a move prints its ETA and suggests a quiet hour; above 500 GB onto the home node it requires --i-mean-it. A 40 GB pull across a 30 ms residential link will otherwise saturate it, and the thing sharing that link is synchronous replication — the one path this document exists to protect. Congestion looks like replication lag, which looks like a network fault. Cost: rebuilds take longer.
Is nosync on the right Pod? doctor measures an RTT matrix and warns on two conditions: the tagged Pod is not the slowest member, or the spread between fastest and slowest is under 10 ms and the tag is buying nothing. The tag encodes a physical fact that changes when you move a VPS, and nobody re-reads a YAML comment after a hardware migration. A warning beats a remembered rule. Cost: a probe between every pair of hosts on a timer, which is also how you would notice an MTU problem.
How much will this carry? A written envelope: 8 Pods per host, 8 clusters, 24 Pods total, 7 etcd members. status prints the port budget against those limits, and create refuses past them rather than warning. Two ports per Pod is 16 ports at the limit of the per-host allowance, leaving room inside 21000-21999 (the range itself permits far more — the envelope is policy, not a technical ceiling). Each cluster is three Pods, so 24 Pods is 8 clusters, and each backup job runs on the primary's manager, which is what makes cluster count a scheduling limit rather than a DCS limit. Refusing beats warning because the failure at the edge is a port collision on a host nobody is watching. Cost: adding capacity means adding hosts, which is the honest answer.
The DCS availability cliff Five voters, five enrolled hosts, quorum three. Three data hosts plus the app host plus one etcd-only spare. The spare is not a backup server and runs no Pods — it exists to hold a vote, and three losses is a restore from R2. The tempting shortcut stays wrong, which is why it is written here: four voters give quorum 3 and tolerate the same one host loss that three does, so the usual "add a fourth machine" advice buys nothing. Five needs three alive and tolerates two — the only cheap configuration that survives two losses. The correlated risk is what makes paying for it rational: both VPS live in one region, so the home machine plus the spare are what keep you writing when that region or that account misbehaves. Cost: ~$6/month for the spare, and the home machine gets a UPS and auto-start before any optimisation. Beyond five machines, adding voters stops being useful before adding failure domains does.
Do laptops belong on the mesh? Only in a separate device profile that is denied 2379, 7000 and 21000–21999 by network policy. A laptop may run the CLI and nothing else; gateways run on servers. This is the compensating control for "the mesh enrols, it does not authorise" (§02): enrolment stays easy because enrolment no longer confers the ability to reach the control plane. Cost: one extra profile and one policy, and the knowledge that an enrolled laptop is still an enrolled laptop.
What are the actual objectives? Recorded, not estimated: RPO for acknowledged writes 0; RPO for total loss ≤60s; failover ≈40s; restore targeted at 30 minutes for ≤100 GB on a VPS, and honestly hours onto the home machine — measured by the drill either way. 0 and 60s follow from strict synchronous replication and archive_timeout: 60. The restore figure is the only one that is an estimate, and it is deliberately location-dependent: 100 GB at residential bandwidth is hours, not minutes, so the drill records which host it ran on alongside the wall-clock time. A cluster is PROVISIONAL in status until verify --full passes once. Cost: one drill before you call anything production.
Third-copy provider (tier 3) Offered once at cluster create; the answer is recorded in the spec. Declined is a permanent answer, not a nag. It is the only tier covering a Cloudflare-wide outage, and it costs almost nothing, so it deserves an offer — but making it mandatory would put a second provider account in the path of "get a database running", which is the opposite of the goal. Cost: one prompt, one reminder you have already answered.
failsafe_mode On. With the documented caveat: it relaxes some DCS error paths, and an etcdserver: no leader condition has been reported to bypass it. A partial mesh failure that costs a storefront thirty seconds is the failure we most expect from a home connection, and failsafe_mode is the only thing that addresses it. It does not change the total outage case, so it is not being relied on for safety. Cost: a slightly wider set of "am I still leader" conditions to reason about.
Where does a human read a secret? Printed once at create into ~/.pgdbaas/credentials (0600) and to the terminal, into your password manager. pgdbaas secret get requires a manager client cert; rotation is cluster set db.password, which re-delivers out of band and never touches the DCS. Every Pod can read the DCS, so a password in the DCS is a password in every container. The passphrase for backups follows the same rule, and losing both directories means restoring from a clean laptop is impossible — which is why bootstrap prints a recovery bundle rather than assuming you wrote it down.
How does a major version upgrade run? Rolling, one Pod at a time, gated on two healthy Pods, into a new storage path. cluster upgrade ecom-db --to 17 takes a fresh full, drains a Pod, runs pg_upgrade, rejoins it, waits for lag zero, moves to the next. Strict sync means you cannot lose the synchronous standby and stay writable, so the gate is real: below two healthy Pods the command refuses instead of degrading. The new path is the §13 retention fix, applied at the moment it matters. Cost: one maintenance window per cluster, and a restore test against the new path the same week.
Gateway fallback Keep the manager API as the only source; no seed fallback. restart: always is mandatory in the emitted compose, and gateway emit warns if you are running only one gateway. Adding the DCS as a second source gives the gateway two ways to fail and doubles what it must be configured with. The realistic window — a gateway starting while every manager is down — fails loud and restarts within seconds. A second gateway is the fix for the case that matters, which is a host going away, not a resync failing.
Connection pooling PgBouncer in front of HAProxy, in the same container, one app-facing port. pool_mode = transaction, 1000 client connections onto 25 server ones, with a reserve pool of 5 held back for the reconnect burst after a failover. HAProxy is loopback-only behind it. PostgreSQL's default of 100 connections fails in the two situations this document spends the most time on: a spike, and a failover reconnect storm — both while the database is perfectly healthy. Order matters: role selection belongs in HAProxy, and putting HAProxy in front would need a PgBouncer reload at exactly the wrong moment. Cost: transaction pooling means per-session SET leaks between clients, so statement timeouts move to the role or the database.
The read path Split by database name on the same port: /ecom writes, /ecom_ro reads. Catalogue and listing traffic is routed to replicas; anything a user writes and then immediately reads stays on /ecom. One connection string is the difference between a read split that people actually use and one that sits unwired in a config file — and the two Pods that are not the writer are otherwise doing nothing during a spike. Deliberately not offered as "reads are safe to lag": read-your-writes does not hold across a replay gap, and an order confirmation that can't find the order costs more than the load it saved. Cost: two pools to size, and one thing to explain to whoever writes the queries.
Your application (out of scope) Not designed here, deliberately. This project owns the database layer: Pods, replication, the gateway, backups and recovery. Your application owns itself. §04 records the two things it needs from you — a pool that retries on connect, and one idempotency key per checkout — because the guarantees above depend on them, but nothing here configures your app for you. Why a boundary and not a feature: an architecture that reaches into your application owns your application, and the database layer is the piece nobody else will own for you. The two requirements are still not optional — strict sync is durable but not atomic (§09), so a retried charge is possible without an idempotency key, and a pool without retries turns a forty-second failover into a down page. Cost: two lines of configuration and one test during a switchover, which is exactly as far as this project should reach.
Certificate expiry Device certificates valid 90 days, renewed by the manager when a third of the life is left, with doctor warning at 14 days. The CA is good for five years and is archived with the recovery bundle. Emergency path: node reissue --local, run on the host itself, which needs no mesh and no CLI from outside. The failure this prevents is the worst kind: everything green, then on some unannounced Tuesday the managers cannot authenticate to the DCS and every cluster stops reconciling. Certificates never announce themselves the way a disk does. Cost: a renewal loop in the manager, and the discipline that the recovery bundle — CA, private keys, backup passphrase — lives in your password manager, because a clean-laptop restore is impossible without it.
One thing still genuinely open, on purpose

The measured restore time. Every other number here is derived from a setting, but "how long until the application is serving from this database again after you lose everything" is a function of your data size and your link, and it is only knowable by doing it. The monthly drill produces it. Until the first one reports, the honest answer stays an estimate — and the cluster is marked PROVISIONAL until then.
